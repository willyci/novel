0.01 Degree
Prologue: The Drift
In the silent, witnessless void of space, an unassuming asteroid collides with a celestial neighbor. The impact is minor in cosmic terms—a mere shudder in the dark—yet it shears away several fragments. One small piece, a mote of unremarkable stone, is jolted onto a new trajectory toward the inner solar system. It tumbles onward, cold and unnoticed, through the vastness of the vacuum. It is a bit player in a drama that has no audience, carrying within it the weight of an extinction it does not know it will cause.“A drama unfolding in the vast indifference of the universe. In the grand cosmic ballet, it plays but a tiny, forgettable role—one of countless bits of matter scattered across the indifferent universe.”
Chapter 1: Echoes of Extinction
Sarah Parker stood motionless before the colossal Tyrannosaurus Rex skull at Earth’s Natural History Museum. A Martian historian by training, she carried a private grief into every lecture: the loss of her husband and her son, Ben, both of whom had shared a love for Earth's extinct fauna. For the class of Martian-born children behind her, this was a pilgrimage to a dead world. Their bodies, sculpted by generations of low gravity and radiation shielding, were living testaments to human persistence.The lecture was interrupted by a tall man in a rumpled tweed jacket, his eyes twinkling with an odd, unsettling light. This was the Professor. When Sarah explained the "one-in-a-million" trajectory of the Chicxulub asteroid, the Professor let out a dry, rattling laugh. "What if the asteroid had missed?" he asked. He painted a picture of a lush, verdant paradise—not as a lost wonder, but as a stolen future. To him, the rise of humanity was not a triumph, but a biological error that corrected a much more beautiful reality. Sarah found herself unable to dismiss him; her grief made her susceptible to his "paradise lost" rhetoric, and the vision of a world that never fell lingered with her long after he vanished into the crowd.
Lore Integration: The Ruined Earth  Old Earth is no longer the cradle of life, but a scavenged shell. After the Great Environmental Collapse, the planet was hollowed out for its remaining minerals. It remains a graveyard of industrial scars, serving only as a grim museum of what happens when a species outgrows its planet’s carrying capacity. The Natural History Museum stands among the ruins as one of the last preserved sites—the destination of school pilgrimages from Mars, where children come to see what was lost.

Chapter 2: The Micro-Heist (The Missing Nanomachines)
At a sprawling logistics warehouse, Steve and Bob were finishing a routine inventory of asteroid mining hardware. Bob was frantic, checking his watch; he had a date with Mary and was desperate to leave. Steve, however, found a hairline fracture in a glass tube. To the naked eye, the tube was empty. In reality, it contained thousands of dormant, self-replicating nanomachines."They're designed to be fired from a cannon, like a bullet," Steve explained, his voice hushed. "One tube hits an asteroid, and the machines do the rest—mine, replicate, refine."Later that night, Steve met a smuggler named Joe in the Martian shadows. The trade was an exercise in irony: Steve handed over the stolen nanomachines—technology capable of reshaping a planet—in exchange for a forbidden, biological pet: a hamster. Steve cradled the creature, mesmerized by its "tiny ears," risking his life for one small heartbeat. Meanwhile, Joe, clinical and pragmatic, warned Steve to burn all DNA traces. Joe viewed the hamster as a cable-chewing nuisance; Steve viewed it as a reason to live.
Chapter 3: Acquisition of the Accelerator
The Professor, using the alias "Dr. Freeman," infiltrated the Mars Energy Research Laboratory. He navigated the bureaucracy with practiced ease, citing his association with a "Dr. Hadron"—a former colleague whose name still carried weight in the facility. His goal: a legacy particle accelerator capable of the high-energy bursts required to tear the fabric of spacetime.

Chapter 4: Recruiting Peter (The Two-Layered Cover Story)
Peter, a math genius and navigation programmer, was desperate. He had been displaced by AI navigators—disposable silicon pilots that didn't require oxygen or salary. When the Professor’s group approached him, they offered a salvation built on a sophisticated deception.Operational Briefing: The "Zero-Wait Supply Chain" (Layer 2 Lie)
The Proposition:  Peter was told he was building a revolutionary mining system.
The "Mining" Logic:  The group claimed they would send nanomachines 67 million years into the past via a micro-wormhole. These machines would spend eons mining the asteroid belt and refining ingots.
The Economic Incentive:  By launching the refined cargo on a million-year trajectory calculated to arrive in 2543, the company would have a "Zero-Wait" supply. They would simply "catch" the ingots as they arrived in the present day.
The Trap:  Peter initially bought the greed-driven logic, but his genius proved dangerous when he began questioning why the star charts required such massive celestial displacement math.
Chapter 5: The Stolen Sun 2.0
The group struck the National Energy Laboratory on Mars to secure "Sun 2.0," an experimental high-density energy cell. The heist was violent. A massive explosion rocked the facility, killing Mike, a security guard. In the aftermath, Lab Director Michael Jones issued a public statement claiming "100% cooperation" with investigators, a PR move to mask the catastrophic nature of the theft.Lore Integration: Exotic Matter  To keep the throat of a wormhole open, the group utilized "Exotic Matter" with negative energy density. This hypothetical substance prevents the gravitational collapse of the micro black hole, allowing the nanomachine payload to be "cannon-launched" into the past. Following the heist, the group fled the inner solar system, hiding in a hollowed-out asteroid in the Kuiper Belt to begin the final phase.
Lore Integration: The Kuiper Belt Frontier  The belt is a lawless expanse of mining rigs and processing plants—the only place where a group can hide a project of this scale. The Secret Lab, located within a hollowed-out asteroid at the solar system's edge, houses the stolen particle accelerator and the Sun 2.0 reactor. Around it sprawls a mining culture of high-risk logistics, where the difference between a payday and a death sentence is a single cracked glass tube.
Chapter 6: The Discovery and the Sabotage
Peter’s suspicions peaked when he realized the navigation algorithms weren't for cargo—they were a targeting package for the Chicxulub asteroid. He realized the Professor didn't want minerals; he wanted to delete humanity.Under the guise of optimizing the AI’s core logic, Peter—the math genius—embedded a masterpiece of obfuscated code. He created a logical conditional: if the AI’s sensors confirmed it was in the distant past, it was to apply a -0.01 degree nudge instead of the +0.01 degree target. He hid this "bug" within the gravitational parallax calculations, knowing the AI would prioritize sensor data over its initial mission parameters once the maneuver began.
Chapter 7: The Event Horizon
On the Kuiper Belt base, the accelerator hummed with the stolen power of Sun 2.0. The Professor watched with a cryptic smile as a quantum-mechanical black hole was torn open.To stabilize the transit, the group used highly focused laser beams to trap and manipulate the atoms of the nanomachine payload, holding them in a precise electromagnetic field gradient at the focal point of the wormhole. The single nanomachine was then "cannon-launched" through the higher-dimensional spacetime. It emerged 67 million years in the past, a microscopic invader in a pristine era.
Chapter 8: The Million-Year Iteration
Emerging in the late Cretaceous, the nanomachine began its "busy work." It drifted until it impacted a mineral-rich asteroid. Using solar energy and hydrogen extracted from water ice, it began to replicate.
Centuries 1-5:  Exponential replication of the microscopic workforce.
Centuries 6-50:  Hollowing out a massive asteroid to serve as a protective shell, shielding the growing system from cosmic rays.
The Result:  The system evolved into a fully autonomous satellite, an apex predator of physics equipped with a high-thrust ion propulsion system and an AI capable of "million billions of calculations."
Chapter 9: The 0.01 Degree Failure
As the Chicxulub asteroid entered the inner solar system, the satellite engaged. It performed a parallax check against Jupiter and the Sun. However, Peter’s bug forced the satellite to the "Shadow Side" of the asteroid.
The Gravity Tractor Maneuver:  Rather than a kinetic impact, the satellite was designed to fly alongside the target asteroid. Its own mass exerts a "tiny gravity" pull over decades, intended to shift the rock’s trajectory by exactly 0.01 degrees—enough to make it miss Earth. The maneuver demands perfect positioning: the same gravitational whisper that can save a world can, from the wrong side, condemn it.

By the time the satellite emerged from the asteroid's shadow to re-calculate, the "0.01-degree" window of influence had closed. Orbital mechanics had become destiny.
Chapter 10: The Professor’s Final Stand
In 2543, the radical group watched their temporal sensors. The timeline remained stagnant. Humanity still existed. The lab was soon breached by authorities, alerted by the energy signature of the transit.The Professor stood amidst the blinking lights, his eyes gleaming with renewed malice. "A temporary setback," he whispered, dismissing the failure and already devising a new plan for eradication. He viewed the persistent human race as a virus that had survived a cure. Peter, however, watched the news of the Martian children at the museum and felt a quiet victory. His sabotage hadn't just saved his species; it had ensured that the "Age of Mammals" was the intended outcome of a billion-to-one shot. He had embedded a "Message for Help" in the satellite’s long-range transmission code, a final hope that someone, someday, would understand the cost of their existence.

Final Synthesis: Ethical & Philosophical Conclusion
The "0.01 Degree" error stands as the ultimate testament to the limits of technological hubris. The Professor’s group sought to play God, but they were thwarted by the very thing they despised: human morality and the chaotic complexity of the universe. The resilience of the timeline suggests that history possesses a momentum that cannot be easily derailed by a fractional degree—as if the universe possesses a "chronological immune system," where the human element, the very thing the group hated, is the one variable that can never be fully calculated. Even with the power to harness black holes and project influence across eons, they were undone by a single line of code and the unyielding geometry of the solar system. While Peter’s sabotage preserved civilization, it also preserved the species' capacity for self-destruction. The transition from the Age of Reptiles to the Age of Mammals was a matter of a single, calculated error, proving that the foundation of our entire history is as fragile as a hairline fracture in a glass tube.
